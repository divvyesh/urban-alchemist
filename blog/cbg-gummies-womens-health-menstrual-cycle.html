CBG, gummies
& your cycle.

Early research. Real questions. A closer look at CBG, edibles and menstrual health.

If cramps, restless nights or premenstrual changes have you reading CBG gummy labels, you deserve an answer that takes both your symptoms and the evidence seriously. CBG is being studied in people, but research has not established it as a treatment for menstrual cramps, PMS, PMDD or endometriosis. The small amount of cycle-related evidence needs much more testing.[5][7][9]

This guide looks at the research available through October 27, 2026. It focuses on women who menstruate, while recognizing that some women do not have periods and some people who menstruate are not women. Its purpose is to help you ask informed questions about symptoms, labels and care.

What CBG is, and how it differs from CBD and THC

Cannabigerol, usually shortened to CBG, is one of the cannabinoids found in cannabis. It is chemically distinct from cannabidiol (CBD) and tetrahydrocannabinol (THC). These names are not interchangeable: research on one compound does not automatically tell us what another will do.[1][2]

THC is the main compound responsible for the cannabis high. CBD does not produce that characteristic high. CBG is also often described as non-intoxicating, and small human studies have not found the same pattern of intoxication associated with THC. That description is narrower than a promise of no side effects or impairment under every circumstance.[1][2][3]

When a package combines cannabinoids, the whole formula matters. A CBG label does not tell you, by itself, whether the product also contains THC, CBD or other active ingredients. Read the ingredient and cannabinoid information rather than inferring the contents from the largest word on the front.

A gummy is a format, not clinical proof

The symptom and safety studies discussed here used particular tinctures, an oil preparation or tablets. Their findings cannot simply be transferred to every gummy, chocolate or other edible carrying the letters CBG.[2][3][4]

There is human research on CBG gummies: a small absorption study found that meal conditions and formulation affected blood exposure. It did not establish a menstrual benefit. More compound reaching the bloodstream is not the same finding as better health, and the experiment does not justify advice to increase absorption.[17]

To judge a claim, ask whether researchers tested the same ingredients, formulation and route of administration, in people with the same problem, for long enough to answer the question. A study of short-lived anxiety in healthy adults is different from a study of recurrent menstrual pain. Neither an attractive label nor the fact that something is swallowed closes that gap.

This is also why a testimonial can be meaningful to the person sharing it without predicting your experience. It tells you what that person noticed. A controlled trial asks the additional question: did the tested treatment produce a greater improvement than the comparison treatment?

For a useful cycle-focused trial, we would want clearly defined symptoms, a suitable comparison group and follow-up across repeated cycles. We would also want to know about hormonal treatments and other medicines, and whether changes improved daily functioning. Feeling different for an hour and missing fewer workdays because of cramps are different outcomes. A headline should tell you which one was measured.

What the controlled human studies actually found

A 2024 placebo-controlled crossover study tested a CBG tincture in 34 healthy adults with cannabis experience, including 18 women. A simple anxiety rating favored CBG, but the validated state-anxiety questionnaire did not show a clear treatment advantage. Overall stress and mood results were not significantly different; one early stress measurement favored CBG. The study was remote, brief and did not investigate menstrual symptoms.[2]

Those mixed results justify further research. They do not establish treatment for an anxiety disorder, premenstrual mood symptoms or PMDD. Including women in a study is valuable, but it does not make the study a test of cycle-related effects.

A newer 2026 laboratory study examined oral CBG in 12 healthy adults. Researchers found no robust overall psychological or physiological effects and reported no drug-related adverse events during the acute experiment. It helped characterize short-term responses and absorption, rather than demonstrating ongoing symptom relief or reproductive safety.[3]

Sleep findings also warrant restraint. A randomized veteran study assigned 63 people to CBG tablets or placebo. Fifty-five completed the study, and 35 met the requirements for its per-protocol analysis. CBG did not significantly outperform placebo for sleep, quality of life or PTSD symptoms, and wearable sleep measurements showed no treatment advantage. Most participants were men, and other cannabis use was permitted. The authors reported industry relationships.[4]

These studies address useful questions, but none establishes a CBG gummy routine for sleep or mood changes around a period. A possible benefit remains a question to investigate, not an outcome to promise.

The cycle-specific evidence is very small

It helps to separate the questions before looking at the numbers. Menstrual cramps concern pain around menstruation. PMS describes a pattern of premenstrual symptoms, while PMDD involves especially disruptive mood symptoms. Evidence about one does not establish treatment for the others. A calmer afternoon, for example, would not by itself demonstrate improvement in a recurring premenstrual disorder.[12][14]

A survey of 127 people using CBG-predominant cannabis included six who reported using it for menstrual cramps, five for PMS, three for endometriosis and one for PMDD. Respondents described perceived improvements, but those tiny groups cannot establish how reliably a treatment works.[5]

There was no randomized comparison, and the products were not necessarily CBG alone. The authors highlighted selection and recall bias, possible placebo effects, and uncertainty about the contributions of other cannabinoids. Someone who tried a product without benefit may also be less likely to appear in a survey of its users.[5]

People have reported benefits, but those reports do not demonstrate that CBG caused them. These experiences offer reasons to design better trials. They do not establish a cycle-regulating effect, a fertility benefit, hormone balancing or a phase-by-phase CBG schedule. They also cannot tell us whether a particular retail edible will help an individual reader.

Laboratory findings and an ongoing study

A 2025 experiment examined several cannabinoids, including CBG, in an endometrial stromal cell line. Researchers observed changes in receptor-related signaling. Cells in a laboratory are not a person experiencing cramps, trying to conceive or managing endometriosis. These findings do not demonstrate symptom relief, hormone balance, improved fertility or infertility in humans.[6]

The Women’s Health Initiative at MIND lists an ongoing study of a full-spectrum CBD–CBG vaginal suppository for endometriosis. Its investigator describes an eight-week study examining absorption, biological responses and symptoms. Results were not available in the sources reviewed for this article.[7]

That work matters because it asks questions missing from the current evidence. It still studies a combined formulation through a different route from a gummy. Even a future positive result would need to be interpreted in that specific context.

CBD findings should stay in their own column

Some menstrual research concerns CBD rather than CBG. A 2024 study found greater self-reported improvement among people who chose high-CBD suppositories than among those continuing usual treatment. Participants selected their own group; the study was neither randomized nor placebo-controlled.[16]

Stronger designs can produce a different picture. The 2026 DREAMLAND trial randomized 102 participants with endometriosis-related pelvic pain to CBD-enriched oil or placebo. The tested CBD-enriched oil did not outperform placebo for pain. Mild adverse events were more frequent with CBD, and the trial ended after an interim analysis.[8]

Neither result settles the question of CBG. Together, they show why a broad phrase such as “cannabinoids help periods” says more than the evidence allows. ACOG’s gynecologic-pain consensus finds insufficient evidence to recommend cannabis products for these conditions.[9]

Questions to bring to a label—and a clinician

Start with the problem you want help with. Cramping, poor sleep and disabling premenstrual depression call for different assessments. Writing down the symptom and its timing can make a conversation more useful than asking whether a product is broadly “good for women.”

  • What is actually in it? Look for each cannabinoid, other active ingredients, allergens and serving information.
  • What does the evidence match? Was this formulation studied for your specific concern, or does the claim borrow from a different compound or laboratory experiment?
  • What can testing establish? Ask whether a report matches the batch and which substances were tested. A composition report cannot demonstrate that the product treats a medical condition.
  • What remains uncertain? “No high” does not answer questions about interactions, repeated use, pregnancy or treatment benefit.

For stress or sleep, the relevant question is whether CBG adds a demonstrated benefit to an appropriate care plan; current small trials have not settled that. For period pain or PMDD, the question begins with diagnosis and established options. Research participation is another distinct setting, with a protocol, eligibility assessment and monitoring.

Safety needs more than a non-intoxicating label

The short CBG studies cannot establish long-term safety. In the veteran trial, possible product-related adverse events included headache, lethargy, stomach upset, nausea and excessive sleepiness. A small acute study reporting no drug-related events cannot rule out uncommon problems or effects that emerge with repeated use.[3][4]

Tell a clinician or pharmacist about the complete formula and your medicines and supplements. CBD has documented interaction and liver-injury concerns; those findings should not be presented as proof of identical CBG risks. They do matter when CBD is part of a mixed product. The limited CBG studies do not establish compatibility with other medicines.[3][10]

Reproductive safety is another unresolved question. FDA advises avoiding CBD, THC and marijuana during pregnancy and breastfeeding. That guidance does not establish CBG-specific safety, and the absence of reliable CBG pregnancy data is not reassurance.[10]

If you are trying to conceive, pregnant or breastfeeding, discuss cannabinoid use and alternatives with your care team. ACOG recommends avoiding cannabis, while noting that continued use alone is not a contraindication to breastfeeding. Seek individualized support rather than abruptly changing a feeding plan or stopping prescribed treatment.[11]

Your symptoms deserve care while research catches up

For period pain, established options include heat, gentle activity and appropriate pain medicines; clinicians may also discuss hormonal contraception. Which approach fits depends on your health, preferences and pregnancy plans. Pain does not have to become unbearable before you ask for help.[12]

Endometriosis care can include analgesics, hormonal treatment and surgery, with decisions shaped by symptoms and fertility goals. CBG should not be presented as a substitute for assessment or a treatment shown to remove endometriosis lesions.[13]

PMS and PMDD are also different from cramps. If premenstrual mood changes repeatedly disrupt work, relationships or daily life, tell a clinician. Evidence-based PMDD options include SSRIs, certain hormonal contraceptives and cognitive behavioral therapy. Tracking symptoms can help clarify the pattern.[14]

Arrange assessment for pain that interferes with usual activities, worsening or heavier periods, bleeding between periods, or pain with sex, urination or bowel movements. Severe pain that is worse than usual and not relieved by pain medicines warrants urgent advice.[12]

At an appointment, useful questions include: What might explain this pattern? What would each treatment aim to improve? How will we judge whether it is helping? What should prompt an earlier review? Bring the product label if cannabinoids are part of the conversation. You should be able to discuss what you have tried without having your symptoms dismissed or being expected to arrive with a diagnosis.

Seek emergency care for severe or worsening pelvic pain, or pelvic pain with fainting, shoulder-tip pain, breathing difficulty or heavy vaginal bleeding. Pelvic pain with possible pregnancy also needs prompt assessment.[15]

You can be curious about CBG without accepting promises the research cannot support. The most useful next step is a clearer understanding of your symptoms, a careful look at the exact formula, and a care plan grounded in what is known.
